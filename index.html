Outlook & Positioning

The S&P 500 finished the quarter near its record while bonds had their worst quarter since 1994, so a client who started the year at 60/40 has drifted toward equities without making a decision, just as relative value has swung toward bonds. With the stock-bond correlation positive, bonds are earning their keep as a source of return, not yet as a hedge.

  • Rebalance to target, in stages. Stage bond purchases over the coming months; momentum in yields is still strong, so tip-toe in rather than jump off the diving board.
  • Add real yield first. A 10-year TIPS real yield near 2.9% is one of the best real-return anchors of the TIPS era outside 2008; short Treasuries limit duration risk. For taxable clients, munis after their sharpest markdown since 1987.
  • Extend duration on a confirmed top, not before.
  • Respect the narrowness; keep two-sided oil risk funded. When the long end turns, washed-out breadth and ~50% expected 2027 Russell 2000 EPS growth say small caps have the most room to recover.

Risk Assessment

What would make this view wrong, or more costly than expected:

  • Long-end disorder. In a parabolic leg the last 20–30 bp often come fast; a move toward 6% would challenge valuations broadly.
  • AI concentration. A capex or earnings disappointment would hit cap-weighted benchmarks, and both strategies' technology exposure, hardest.
  • Policy error. The Fed could tighten into a slowing economy just as energy prices roll over.
  • Momentum reversal and bitcoin volatility.

Maverick US Large-Cap

September 2026 Reconstitution

Systematic US large-cap strategy — companion report

Brian Hicks, PhD, CFA®, MSF
Partner | Director of Strategy & Portfolio Management

Overview

Maverick is a systematic US large-cap strategy built from two sleeves that do opposite jobs.

The first is a relative-strength sleeve: twenty S&P 500 companies whose share prices have been the strongest over recent months, adjusted for how much risk was taken to get there. It is the engine. It is rebuilt from scratch twice a year, in March and September, and largely left alone in between.

The second is a low-volatility anchor: ten of the calmest names in the index, capped so no single sector can dominate. It is the ballast.

Its job is not to make money in a strong market; its job is to lose less in a weak one. Relative strength investing can be inherently more volatile, so having a “speed governor” attached to the portfolio improves risk adjusted returns over the long run.

All thirty positions are held at equal weight, roughly 3.3% each. Not weighted by conviction, not weighted by score — equally. That is deliberate, as it lowers over concentration risk that can bite hard when this style of investing falls out of favor (i.e. July’s momentum reversal).

Why Price, and Not Opinion

Nothing in the selection rests on a forecast. There is no earnings model, no house view on interest rates, no call on the economy. The strategy measures which companies have been strongest, adjusts that strength by their volatility so a violent mover does not outrank a steady one, and holds the top twenty.

The reasoning is that leadership changes show up in prices before they show up in commentary. By the time it is obvious that capital has moved from one part of the market to another, the price relationship has been saying so for months.

What That Means in Practice

  • It acts twice a year. Names are reconstituted at the start of March and September on the prior month-end signal. Between those dates the book does not change, regardless of the news.
  • It holds on through weakness. A name stays as long as it remains in the top fifty. That buffer stops the strategy churning out of a temporarily soft holding that is still fundamentally leading.
  • It is always fully invested. It does not go to cash and it does not time the market.
  • It will look concentrated when leadership is narrow.

What Changed This September

This was a large reconstitution. Nine of the twenty momentum names rotated out — a 45% turnover of the sleeve, and the most decisive turn since the strategy went live. These were not marginal calls: most of the names leaving had fallen to a rank in the 140–300 range out of roughly 500 eligible companies.

What it sold was the picks-and-shovels side of the AI build-out — the companies making the optical parts, the chip equipment, the data-centre power gear. What it bought was software, security, and a broader mix outside technology. It didn’t predict that shift. It measured it.”

The Energy Decision — Where We Overrode the Model

This is the conversation most worth being ready for, because it is the one place this month where a human judgment sat on top of the system.

The screen wanted to own three oil refiners at once: Valero, Marathon Petroleum and Phillips 66. The model ranks companies one at a time, so it could not see that all three are, in substance, a single bet on refining margins. Three positions would have put roughly 15% of the sleeve into one cycle.

Under a documented override authority — narrow, granted in August, and limited to energy and materials, where the portfolio manager has fifteen years of direct experience — we declined the third. Refining went from three names to two.

The reason to trim rather than add is that refining margins are at a genuine extreme. The US diesel crack spread reached an all-time high above $100 a barrel in August, against a normal range of $15 to $25. Distillate inventories are at their lowest August level since 1951.

“The model wanted to own three oil refiners. It ranks companies individually, so it couldn’t see that all three are really one bet on refining margins. We cut it to two, under a documented authority limited to energy — the one area where we think our own judgment adds something. Diesel margins are at an all-time high on supply disruptions, and those tend to normalise.”

The Anchor Barely Moved

The annual anchor reset produced no changes at all: the ten lowest-volatility names it selected were the ten it already held. One position did change, mechanically. The strategy holds an oil-linked overlay that swaps Exxon in for whichever anchor holding is currently the most volatile, and that name shifted from Federal Realty to Aflac as their volatilities crossed. Aflac out, Federal Realty back in. Exxon itself did not move.

Where This Strategy Belongs

A 45% sleeve turnover in a taxable account is a real tax event. The momentum sleeve belongs in qualified accounts, and that is not a preference — it is how the strategy is designed to be used. Taxable clients are served by the core-satellite structure instead: a tax-efficient core of roughly 80%, with a smaller Maverick allocation alongside it could prove optimal, as a balance between active alpha and tax efficiency.

Neo Macro Portfolio

September 2026 Update

Unconstrained macro portfolio — companion report

Brian Hicks, PhD, CFA®, MSF
Partner | Director of Strategy & Portfolio Management

Overview

Neo is an unconstrained macro portfolio. Its job is to keep client money on the right side of the big economic and market cycles; such as, risk-on vs risk-off regimes, reflation vs disinflation, a strong dollar versus a weak one, value leadership vs growth leadership. Critically, this is achieved without anyone having to forecast (or guess) which way those cycles will turn.

The strategy operates through eight paired sleeves or decision points. Each carefully selected pair sets two opposing investments against one another, and each represents an axis along which markets rotate: US small-cap value against US mega-cap growth, global energy against global technology, commodities against Treasuries, gold against bitcoin, and so on. For each pair the strategy holds one side. Never both, never neither.

Which side it holds is decided entirely by price. The strategy quantitatively measures the trend in the relationship between the two investments — how one has been doing against the other — across four time frames ranging from a quarter out to a full year. It blends those four readings into a single measure and holds whichever side is winning. It reviews all eight at the end of every month and acts only when a relationship has genuinely crossed over. These trends are not high frequency in nature, they can last several months or years. That is the whole method.

What That Means in Practice

  • It is always fully invested — eight positions, roughly one-eighth each, plus a small cash residual. It does not go to cash and it does not time the market.

Current Positioning

Line them up and a picture emerges. The strategy is currently favouring smaller companies over mega-caps, value over growth, international over defensive global equity, and real assets over bonds. That is, almost exactly, the rotation that has defined 2026 — capital broadening out of the handful of enormous US technology names that dominated the preceding years.

It got there without predicting anything. It got there because those trends showed up in prices and the model followed them.

Why Small-Cap Value Is Leading — The Standout Position

IWN, the US small-cap value position, is the strongest holding in the portfolio and the only one where every measurement horizon agrees, from three months out to a full year. It won its contest against US mega-cap growth, and it won convincingly.

That lines up with what has been the story of the year. The Russell 2000 has been having its best run in more than two decades, and small-cap value in particular has been taking leadership from the large technology names that carried the market for years.

The Tension: ~40% Technology

Here is the tension, and it’s worth understanding rather than glossing over. Four of the eight positions are still on the technology side of their contest: global technology, semiconductors, Nasdaq-100 growth, and US momentum, which is itself heavily weighted toward technology. On a look-through basis the portfolio is roughly 40% technology.

So Neo is currently holding both sides of an argument. Half the book expresses the rotation into value, international and real assets. The other half is still riding the technology complex that the rotation is supposedly leaving behind. Both trends have been real, and both have been strong enough to win their contests.

What’s changed recently is that the shorter-term readings across the technology positions have started to soften, while the value and commodity positions have held or improved. Nothing has crossed over yet. But that’s the direction of travel, and it’s the honest answer to a client who asks whether the portfolio is going to sit at 40% technology indefinitely.

“The portfolio is technology-heavy — around 40% when you look through the funds. That’s a consequence of following trends, and technology has been leading for a long time. What we’re watching is that the most recent readings on those positions have started to soften while the value and commodity side has held up. Nothing has flipped yet, but that’s the direction.”

Energy vs. Technology — The Closest Contest

This is the conversation you are most likely to have this month, because oil is in the headlines and clients will connect the dots themselves.

The setup: One of the eight contests is global energy against global technology, and it is currently the closest of the eight — close enough that it could flip in either direction. Meanwhile Brent has pushed above $100 on Iran-related supply disruption, and US refined product inventories, diesel in particular, are at record seasonal lows. It looks like an obvious trade.

We looked at it seriously and decided against making the switch ahead of the signal. Two pieces of work informed that:

  • First, we tested whether adding an oil-price trigger to that decision would have helped historically — it wouldn’t have, and it performed worst on the very position it was designed to help.
  • Second, we looked at every previous occasion this exact configuration appeared over twenty-five years: energy rallying hard over three months while still trailing over six. It has usually been a bounce rather than a turn, and acting early has typically cost money.

The most important part of that answer, for a client, is what it says about how we operate.

Gold Out, Bitcoin In

After three months without a trade, the September 30 signal tipped the gold-versus-bitcoin pair: Neo sold gold (GLDM) and bought bitcoin (IBIT), executed October 1. This was the first trade since June and reflects the model’s reading that bitcoin’s relative trend has crossed above gold’s across the blended time horizons.
